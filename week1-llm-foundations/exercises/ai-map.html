  2. 2

    What each one does, how it works, and where you've met it

    AI

    the umbrella · a field since 1956

    What it doesComputers doing tasks that usually need human thinking: reasoning, perceiving, deciding.

    How it worksTwo families of method. Early AI used rules a person wrote, and search: trying many possible moves and picking the best. Most AI today learns from data instead; that's every layer below.

    You've met itChess engines, the shortest route in a maps app, characters in games.

    At your workRule-based chatbots and “expert systems”, and everything in the layers below.

    Machine learning

    part of AI · named in 1959

    What it doesLearns patterns from examples instead of hand-written rules, and gets better with more data.

    How it worksYou show it many examples with the right answer (spam, not spam) and it finds the pattern itself. It comes in three flavours: learning from labelled examples, finding groups or oddities without labels, and learning by trial and reward.

    You've met itThe spam folder, “you may also like”, your bank's “was this you?” fraud alert.

    At your workAlarms that learn a metric's normal daily pattern and flag the odd spike (AWS CloudWatch's anomaly detection is one); traffic forecasts for capacity planning; auto-tagging tickets.

    Deep learning

    part of machine learning · took off in 2012

    What it doesMachine learning with many-layered neural networks. It learns straight from photos, sound and raw text.

    How it worksA neural network is layers of very simple maths units, each with adjustable numbers. Training nudges the numbers to make fewer mistakes, and each layer learns something a little more abstract than the one before: edges, then shapes, then faces. It needs lots of data and powerful chips (GPUs).

    You've met itFace unlock, voice typing, translation, searching your photos for “beach”.

    At your workTranscribing support calls, reading scanned forms (OCR), spotting defects in camera images on a production line.

    Generative AI

    part of deep learning · mainstream since 2022

    What it doesCreates new content (text, images, code, audio) instead of only sorting or scoring.

    How it worksTrained on huge amounts of data, it learns the patterns well enough to produce new examples of them. Most are foundation models: trained once on broad data, then used for many different tasks.

    You've met itChatGPT and Claude writing, image generators, AI voices.

    At your workDrafting a status update, generating test data, suggesting code.

    LLM: large language model

    the part of generative AI that writes text

    What it doesA huge model trained on vast amounts of text to predict the next token (a chunk of a word). It's the engine inside ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

    How it worksIt guesses the next token, adds it, and guesses again. It's trained first to predict text, then to follow instructions. It predicts what sounds right, not what is true: it isn't looking anything up.

    You've met itChat assistants, “summarise this” buttons, smart replies.

    At your workSummarising an incident timeline, triaging a ticket (that's Lab 3), explaining a runbook. This course works here.

    Next level up: the agent

    an LLM with tools and a loop · Weeks 4–5

    What it doesDoes a whole job, not just one answer.

    How it worksIt plans a step, uses a tool (search, run a command, query a database), reads the result, and repeats until the job is done. A person approves the risky steps.

    You've met itCoding agents such as Claude Code and Copilot's agent mode (Topic 7's live demo).

    At your workAn on-call helper that reads the logs and suggests the next check. You'll build one in Week 4.

    Take awayPeople write rules. Machine learning finds its own rules in examples. Deep learning does that on photos, sound and raw text. Generative AI makes new things. An LLM is the one that writes text.
  3. 3

    Rules, patterns or next words? Sort these six

    For each one, decide which box it belongs in before you open the answer.

    “If disk usage is over 90%, page the on-call.”

    Plain software, not AI. One rule a person wrote: same input, same output, every time. Exactly right for alerting.

    “Flag a login from a new country at 3 am as suspicious, based on millions of past logins.”

    Machine learning. Nobody wrote that rule; the system learned what normal looks like from examples.

    “Turn this recorded support call into text.”

    Deep learning (speech-to-text). Neural networks learned to hear words in raw sound.

    “Write a polite reply to this angry customer email.”

    Generative AI, and specifically an LLM. It creates new text, one token at a time.

    “Find the fastest route to the data centre.”

    Classic AI: search, trying routes and picking the best, helped by machine learning that predicts the traffic.

    “Answer questions from a 200-page datasheet.”

    An LLM plus retrieval (RAG): find the right pages first, then answer from them. That's Week 3.

    Take awayReal systems mix all three: rules where you need certainty, an LLM where you need language, and tests around both.
  4. 4

    When each one arrived

    1. 1956AI becomes a field, at a summer workshop at Dartmouth College in the USA. The name “artificial intelligence” comes from the proposal for it.
    2. 1959Arthur Samuel at IBM names machine learning: his checkers program got better by playing against itself.
    3. 2012Deep learning wins the ImageNet picture-recognition contest by a wide margin (a network called AlexNet), and the boom starts.
    4. 2017Researchers at Google publish the Transformer (“Attention Is All You Need”): the design behind today's LLMs.
    5. 2022ChatGPT launches on 30 November. Generative AI goes mainstream.
    6. 2025Coding agents: Anthropic's Claude Code becomes generally available in May. You'll see it in Topic 7, and build an agent of your own in Weeks 4–5.
    Take awayThe inner box is young: ChatGPT turns four on 30 November 2026.
  5. 5

    More words you'll hear

    Token
    A chunk of text, about 4 characters in English. Models read, write, bill and limit in tokens (Topic 3).
    Parameters
    The adjustable numbers inside a model. Training sets them; your questions don't change them.
    Training vs using
    Training builds the model once, over months, at huge cost. Using it (called inference) is every question you send.
    Fine-tuning
    Extra training on your own examples to specialise a model. This course doesn't do it: better prompts and retrieval come first.
    Prompt
    The instructions and text you send the model (Topic 6).
    Context window
    Everything the model can see in one call. It starts empty on every call (Topic 3).
    Hallucination
    A fluent, confident, wrong answer (Topic 6).
    Foundation model
    One big model trained on broad data, then used for many tasks.
    Multimodal
    Reads (or writes) more than text, such as images and audio. Claude can read images.
    Open-weight
    A model whose trained numbers you can download and run yourself. Read its licence first.
    GPU
    A chip that does many calculations at once. Models are trained and run on GPUs and similar chips.
    RAG
    Retrieval-augmented generation: find the relevant text first, then answer from it (Week 3).
    Agent
    An LLM that uses tools in a loop until a job is done (Weeks 4–5).
    Take awayNo need to memorise these: come back when a word stops you. The deck's last pages have the course glossary.

Quick check

Answer in your head or on paper first, then open the answer. Not graded. If one surprises you, ask in the cohort WhatsApp group.

  1. Is a spam filter generative AI?

    Answer

    No. It's machine learning: it sorts emails into spam or not spam. It doesn't create anything.

  2. An alert fires when CPU is over 90% for five minutes. Is that AI?

    Answer

    No. One rule a person wrote is plain software: same input, same output, every time.

  3. Why can an LLM sound confident and still be wrong?

    Answer

    It predicts what sounds right, not what is true. It isn't looking anything up.

  4. Which box does Lab 3's ticket triage belong in?

    Answer

    The innermost one: an LLM reads the ticket and writes three lines back.

Questions people ask

“Is ChatGPT AI or machine learning?”

Both, and more. It's an app built on an LLM, the innermost box, so it's also generative AI, deep learning, machine learning and AI.

“Does it learn from what I type?”

Not while it answers you: its numbers are fixed. Whether a company later trains new models on your chats depends on its terms and your settings. Check before you paste anything sensitive, and never paste passwords, keys or client data.

“What's AGI?”

Artificial general intelligence: a hypothetical AI as capable as people at most tasks. Nobody agrees on a definition or a date, and nothing in this course depends on it.

“Does it understand?”

It predicts text extremely well. Whether that counts as understanding is debated; in this course we judge it by what it does, and we measure that.

“Will AI take my job?”

It changes jobs. Someone still has to decide what the AI should do and check what it did, and that's the skill this course practises.

“Are the boxes exact?”

They're a simplification that works. Some generative models came before deep learning, today's LLMs also read images, and some AI never learns at all.
